What this actually means for the business 84% overseas isn't just an optics problem — the India business, the one the growth story is built on, still runs on cash generated abroad. Reasonable during a transition; harder to justify as a permanent structure. One question worth an answer: can India carry its own weight before that overseas cash flow is needed elsewhere?

What PRISM actually is now — not a hotel company

The DRHP structures the group into three verticals plus adjacent platforms. Read as "OYO Hotels," it's badly understated — and several things that look like open opportunities are in fact businesses PRISM already runs. ~293,500 storefronts across 35+ countries (Dec 2025).

Hotelscore vertical
OYO (budget) · Townhouse & Palette (mid) · Sunday (premium 4–5★, SoftBank JV) · Motel 6 & Studio 6 (US, via G6). ~24,300 hotels.
Homescore vertical
Professionally managed vacation rentals — Belvilla · DanCenter · CheckMyGuest · Studio Prestige. ~124,700 homes.
Listingscore vertical
Subscription product — owners pay a fixed fee to be listed. ~144,600 listings; the purest asset-light, software-like recurring-revenue line in the group, and the closest thing PRISM has to a true "hospitality-OS" revenue stream.
Weddingzcelebration
India's largest wedding-venue & banquet marketplace (acquired 2018, ~19 lakh monthly visitors). Owned already — so weddings are an existing line, not a greenfield entry.
Innov8workspaces
Co-working / workspace platform inside the group — plus tours, events and F&B offerings noted in the DRHP.
Technologyenabling layer
AI-driven partner tools and a data-science platform PRISM already markets publicly — an existing capability, not a missing strategy.
Execution, not entry — the bar this sets For a platform this wide, the strategic question isn't which new business to enter — it's whether each vertical it already runs earns its capital and its share of management attention. Weddings, residences, hospitality tech and AI aren't whitespace to move into; they're operating lines that have to justify their place. That's a harder test than finding the next adjacency — and the right one for a company asking public markets for ₹6,650 cr.

The Spark

Ritesh Agarwal was 17 when he dropped out of college in Delhi, having stayed in over 100 budget hotels and guesthouses across India and hit the same problem every time: no consistency, no trust in what he'd actually get. Oravel Stays launched in 2012 as a listings aggregator for that segment — and only partly worked, since aggregating listings without controlling quality still left travelers guessing. In 2013 Agarwal became the first Indian selected for the Thiel Fellowship — a $100,000 grant and direct mentorship from Peter Thiel, built specifically for under-20 founders willing to skip college to build full-time. That backing funded the pivot: in May 2013, Oravel relaunched as OYO Rooms, standardizing partner hotels — AC, wifi, breakfast, consistent service — under one brand, the same franchise logic the company still runs today, just at a very different scale.

2–4
The Rise

SoftBank's 2015 investment turned that standardization model into a blitzscaling bet — capital chasing city-count over unit economics. By 2018 that bought unicorn status at $5B; by October 2019 a $1.5B round pushed PRISM to a peak $10B, with Agarwal personally buying ~$2B of shares to raise his own stake — a leveraged vote of confidence right at the top.

5–7
The Fall

Covid didn't just dent the business, it exposed the model: growth funded by capital, not cash flow, collapses the moment travel does. A first IPO attempt was filed in 2021 and quietly shelved in 2022 as institutional holders marked the stake down by roughly 75% from peak. The company spent the next two years converting from a capital-intensive, minimum-guarantee model to an asset-light franchise platform — unglamorous, necessary work.

7–9
The Rebuild

The rebuild wasn't organic growth — it was bought. December 2024's $525M Motel 6 acquisition (the "US deal" marker above) brought a mature, cash-generative American franchise business into the group overnight, while the "EU deal" markers show a parallel, smaller-ticket pattern in Europe. Both moves were debt-funded, and both are now the collateral behind a 2026 IPO priced at $7–8B — recovering, but deliberately still below the 2019 peak.

Competitive Rivalry — High. Treebo, FabHotels, Ginger, and full-service chains are all fighting for the same India footprint. OYO's scale is real, but so is the field — no single player has pricing power.
Threat of New Entrants — Medium. The asset-light franchise model is replicable, and well-funded entrants (Tata's Ginger, OTA-backed brands) can copy it. Brand recognition and existing scale are the main barriers, not capital.
Supplier Power — Medium-High. Independent hotel owners are the "suppliers" here, and switching aggregators or brands is relatively easy for them. If OYO doesn't deliver occupancy and rate uplift, owners can walk.
Buyer Power — High. Price-sensitive travelers and OTAs like MakeMyTrip control much of the demand. Guests have near-zero switching cost, and MakeMyTrip's distribution reach gives it leverage over OYO's own funnel.
Threat of Substitutes — Medium. Direct OTA booking, unbranded budget hotels, and short-term rental platforms all substitute for OYO's core offering, especially at the price-sensitive end.

Why this one can't be a fourth attempt

Two withdrawals already sit on the public record — a third would move past "market conditions" as an explanation and start reading as an execution problem. A close now would also delay liquidity for employees sitting on ESOPs, complicate SoftBank's own path to trimming its ~40% stake, and make the next fundraise — IPO or private — harder to price. The business case has never been stronger; the open question is whether that's enough to finally convert it into a listing.

Weddings — owns the demand, not the venue PRISM isn’t absent from weddings — it has owned Weddingz, India’s largest wedding-venue marketplace, since 2018. But that’s a thin commission layer: it captures the marketplace economics, not the venue economics Taj and Oberoi own. What PRISM alone holds is both sides — the demand (Weddingz) and an emerging premium supply brand (Sunday, now moving into resort and destination markets). Open question: can the two fuse — Weddingz feeding Sunday’s celebration inventory — into one owned-demand-to-supply loop no rival has?

Two ways to close the gap — trade-offs, not a recommendation

Franchise Sunday into venuesRevenue-share with resort/heritage owners near Alwar–Sariska, Ramnagar–Corbett, fed by Weddingz demand. Capital-light; no land upside.
Develop venue real estateBuy land pre-development and build under Sunday — captures land + event revenue. Heavier capex, 2–4yr build. (Goa is now fining unpermitted beach weddings.)

Segments worth exploring — not region-specific

Ideas that could apply across footprints rather than being locked to one bucket

Branded residences vs. managed-living — two different markets Branded residences (Four Seasons / Ritz-style licensing on homes) is a real $30B+ global category — but it’s ultra-luxury, where the brand name carries the premium. A structural mismatch for a budget/mid-market brand.

Managed-living / facility management — housekeeping, concierge, security and property ops for premium societies — is a closer fit: a B2B services model near what OYO already runs. And it’s not greenfield — Studio 6 and Sunday Residences already give PRISM an extended-stay foothold. The question is whether it’s scaled as a deliberate B2B line, or left as a by-product.
The one thread connecting all four Each bucket has its own fix, but they all draw on the same capital and the same management attention — so this only works as a sequence, not four parallel efforts. Group growth past its current ceiling likely hinges on reducing dependence on one acquisition for the scale story.

The surprise wasn’t what PRISM was missing — it was how many of my “new ideas” it had already started. The harder question is rarely which business to invent next, but which existing ones deserve focus and scale.

Why not PRISM?

Musafir Café is a much-loved Hindi novel — now a Netflix series — a story people feel they’ve lived. A musafir, a traveller, is exactly who PRISM hosts every night. So why couldn’t it have its own — a real café that carries a story people seek out, not just a place they pass through? That’s what pulls me: building things people remember, where the experience and the story outlast the stay. Great strategy isn’t about having all the answers — it’s about asking the questions that help everyone find them.
